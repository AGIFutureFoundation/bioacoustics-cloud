# The Models That Listen: BirdNET, Perch, NatureLM, and the Archives Behind Them

## A field guide to the bioacoustics model landscape — what each model really covers, what it costs to use, and what WAVE actually runs today

*An honest status report from WAVE / [bioacoustics.cloud](https://bioacoustics.cloud)*

*Illustration note: no photograph is used in this article. Any illustration added later must be labelled as a generated illustration, not a photograph or real data.*

If you record a dawn chorus, a reef at dusk, or a whale passing a hydrophone, you can now feed that recording to a machine-learning model and get back a list of species, often in seconds, often offline. That is the quiet revolution behind bioacoustics — and a choice that is easy to get wrong, because the models have similar names, overlapping claims, and very different licences, training data, and limits, and because their headline species counts belong to different generations, taxonomic groups, and kinds of task.

This article is a field guide to that landscape: the three models that matter most — [BirdNET](https://birdnet.cornell.edu/), Google's [Perch](https://github.com/google-research/perch), and the Earth Species Project's [NatureLM-audio](https://huggingface.co/EarthSpeciesProject/NatureLM-audio) — and the public archives that trained them: [Xeno-canto](https://xeno-canto.org/), the [Macaulay Library](https://www.macaulaylibrary.org/), the Watkins Marine Mammal Sound Database, [iNaturalist](https://www.inaturalist.org/), the Animal Sound Archive in Berlin, the Earth Species Library, [Project CETI](https://www.projectceti.org/), and [GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/) as the aggregator tying much of it together. Every figure carries a source URL and an as-of date, because the numbers move: archives grow by thousands of recordings a month, and a count correct in March 2025 can mislead by November 2026. Where a figure could not be verified, that is stated plainly instead of filled in.

## What is true, and what is not yet true

The live species predictor on [bioacoustics.cloud](https://bioacoustics.cloud) is [BirdNET](https://birdnet.cornell.edu/) — a model built by Cornell and Chemnitz that identifies birds first. The production model, v2.4 (March 2025), covers 6,522 bird species; the V3.0 generation, in developer preview from June 2026 and in the BirdNET Live app from September 2026, covers 11,000+ species. Those are different generations, not a contradiction. [Perch](https://github.com/google-research/perch) and [NatureLM-audio](https://huggingface.co/EarthSpeciesProject/NatureLM-audio) are **not connected** to WAVE — candidate and comparison models, not integrations that exist today. The archives here are public resources WAVE links to; they are **not owned by WAVE**, and no partnership, integration, or agreement with Cornell, Google, the Earth Species Project, Woods Hole, or any archive is claimed. The public repository for WAVE's own engine, [WAVETranslator/Wild-Animal-Voice-Engine](https://github.com/WAVETranslator/Wild-Animal-Voice-Engine), is a three-file stub — a 154-byte README, a licence, and a gitignore, with a single commit dated 13 June 2026 and no code, no model, and no site source. Nothing here claims any model can translate animal communication, diagnose a habitat, or identify a species with certainty from a single clip. Identification is a probability, not a proof.

## Why three models, not one

It is tempting to ask which model is best and be done with it. The three were built for different jobs. BirdNET is a classifier: give it a recording and it tells you which species are probably in it, with a confidence score, from a fixed list. Perch is a foundation model for transfer learning: give it a recording and it returns a numerical embedding, a compact fingerprint, from which a small classifier can be trained for a new species or task with only a handful of examples. NatureLM-audio is an audio-language model: ask a question in plain English — "Which species are calling here?" — and it answers in words. A classifier answers a closed question well. An embedding model lets you ask new questions cheaply. A language model lets you ask questions in words, including ones nobody trained it to answer. Each strength carries a corresponding weakness, and the rest of this article is an inventory of those trade-offs.

## BirdNET: the working classifier

BirdNET is the model that turned bioacoustics from a specialist craft into something a birder, a ranger, or a school group can use. It comes from the Cornell K. Lisa Yang Center for Conservation Bioacoustics at the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, with the Chair of Media Informatics at Chemnitz University of Technology, and is trained on curated recordings from [Xeno-canto](https://xeno-canto.org/) and the [Macaulay Library](https://www.macaulaylibrary.org/). There are free apps, and the [BirdNET-Analyzer](https://github.com/birdnet-team/birdnet-analyzer) tool for batch analysis of large recording sets. Related technology also powers sound identification in the Merlin app, though Merlin and BirdNET are distinct products.

Two different numbers circulate for BirdNET and both are real. **Version 2.4 is the production model**, released in March 2025: it covers **6,522 bird species**, plus a smaller set of mammals and anurans. That figure is documented in the Zenodo model release of March 2025 and in Funosas and colleagues, *Ecological Indicators* (volume 182, 2026, article 114550), who describe version 2.4 as able to identify more than 6,000 bird species worldwide. The **V3.0 generation**, called BirdNET+ in some materials, is larger: Developer Preview 3.1 was released in June 2026, described as covering 11,000 species, trained on a larger and more diverse dataset with an expanded set of non-bird species. Its repository, [birdnet-team/birdnet-V3.0-dev](https://github.com/birdnet-team/birdnet-V3.0-dev), was created on 10 November 2025 and last committed to on 16 June 2026; its model carries DOI [10.5281/zenodo.20703646](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20703646). The preview warns: "Models, labels, and code will change before final release." The V3.0 FAQ states 9,789 species combined, while the public taxonomy list shows 13,967 species including 11,127 birds — apparently including taxa beyond the current model — and the official About page lists "High species coverage (11k+)" as a design priority. The BirdNET Live app, in Cornell's *SoundBytes* for September 2026, "automatically recognizes 8,927 bird species, 268 mammals, 254 insects, and 340 amphibians, all in real time without an internet or cellular connection," with a user interface in twelve languages, free on Android and iOS.

The honest way to hold all of those numbers at once is to attach each one to its generation and its date. Six thousand five hundred and twenty-two is the v2.4 production-model bird count, March 2025. Eleven thousand and above is the V3.0 generation's headline, June to September 2026, covering birds plus other groups in the preview and live-app builds. Eight thousand nine hundred and twenty-seven birds, plus 268 mammals, 254 insects, and 340 amphibians, is the BirdNET Live app's stated coverage in September 2026. Nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-nine is the V3.0 FAQ's combined species count. None of these is wrong; each is an answer to a slightly different question, and presenting any one of them without its generation and date would mislead. The correct WAVE wording, settled in the research behind this article, is: "BirdNET v2.4 (production, March 2025) covers 6,522 bird species; the V3.0 generation in preview and in live apps covers 11,000+ species." Do not present 11,000+ as the production-model figure, and do not present 6,522 as the current ceiling of what BirdNET can do.

BirdNET's licence and access terms are unusually clear, and they are part of why the model is so widely used. The [BirdNET-Analyzer source code is MIT-licensed](https://github.com/birdnet-team/birdnet-analyzer/blob/HEAD/README.md); the **models themselves are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0** — non-commercial, with educational and research use freely permitted, commercial use requiring a licence from Cornell, and custom models trained with the tool inheriting the same terms. There is **no hosted API**: models and tools are downloadable, and the apps are free. Questions go to ccb-birdnet@cornell.edu. That combination has a practical consequence: you download the model and run it yourself, on your own hardware, under a non-commercial licence unless you have negotiated otherwise. The official repositories, all under [birdnet-team](https://github.com/birdnet-team), include [BirdNET-Analyzer](https://github.com/birdnet-team/BirdNET-Analyzer) (Python, MIT, 1,743 stars, last activity 6 November 2026), [birdnet](https://github.com/birdnet-team/birdnet) (167 stars, 6 November 2026), and the official [birdnet-live-app](https://github.com/birdnet-team/birdnet-live-app) (77 stars, 6 November 2026); [BirdNET-Lite](https://github.com/birdnet-team/BirdNET-Lite) and [BirdNET-V1](https://github.com/birdnet-team/BirdNET-V1) are archived. Alongside Cornell's code sits a vigorous community: [birdnet-go](https://github.com/tphakala/birdnet-go), a community self-hosted analyser written in Go for the Raspberry Pi, had 2,408 stars and was last active on 7 November 2026.

Two gaps remain open and are flagged rather than filled. The final V3.0 release date was still unknown at the time of research — the model was in developer preview as of June 2026 — and the exact non-bird species counts in v2.4 were not verified. A third question is internal, not public: which BirdNET build WAVE actually runs, and whether a V3.0 upgrade is planned. That is a question for the WAVE programme to answer from its own records, not a fact to be asserted from public sources.

## Perch 2.0: the embedding model

If BirdNET is a bird identifier, [Perch](https://github.com/google-research/perch) is a general-purpose ear. It is Google DeepMind and Google Research's bioacoustics foundation model: its job is not to name species directly but to turn any animal sound into an embedding — a numerical fingerprint — from which small classifiers can be trained for new species and tasks with very few examples. That approach, agile modelling, matters because most species have only a handful of clean recordings, too few to train a classifier from scratch but often enough to train a small classifier on a good embedding. Perch is also the basis for [SurfPerch](https://www.kaggle.com/models/google/surfperch), the coral-reef adaptation, and for Google's whale classifiers.

**Perch 2.0 was released on 7 August 2025.** It covers **14,795 classes: 14,597 species plus 198 general sound-event classes** from FSD50K. It produces 1,536-dimensional embeddings from a model of roughly twelve million parameters, in the EfficientNet-B3 class. Its training data, in Table 1 of the paper and downloaded in March and April 2025, totalled **1,542,778 recordings**: 896,255 from Xeno-canto, 571,698 from iNaturalist, 33,859 from the Tierstimmenarchiv (the Animal Sound Archive in Berlin), and 40,966 from FSD50K — a striking concentration, with nearly nine hundred thousand recordings from one community archive. Perch 1.0 (2023) was an EfficientNet-B1 trained on the full Xeno-canto corpus as downloaded in July 2022; DeepMind reports more than 250,000 downloads. On benchmarks, Perch 2.0 reports a BirdSet AUROC of roughly 0.907 to 0.908 and state-of-the-art results on the BEANS tasks; the paper is [arXiv:2508.04665](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.04665), "Perch 2.0: The Bittern Lesson for Bioacoustics," revised January 2026. The paper is candid about a limitation that matters for WAVE's marine ambitions: Perch 2.0 had almost no marine hydrophone training data, only a few dozen cetacean recordings.

Perch's licence is clear at the code level and less fully verified at the weights level. The code in [google-research/perch](https://github.com/google-research/perch) is **Apache-2.0**, though the repository warns parts are out of date and pip installation is unlikely to work; for inference Google directs users to [perch-hoplite](https://github.com/google-research/perch-hoplite) (132 stars, 2 November 2026; the main repo had 403 stars, 6 November 2026). Models are distributed free through **Kaggle Models** — the bird-vocalisation classifier ([kaggle.com/models/google/bird-vocalization-classifier](https://www.kaggle.com/models/google/bird-vocalization-classifier), variant `perch_v2`) and SurfPerch. There is no hosted API. **Perch is not connected to WAVE**, and the Kaggle model page's own licence field was not verified, even though the code is Apache-2.0. No Perch repository exists under `google-deepmind`, and third-party PyTorch ports such as `bghani/perchv2-pytorch` are unofficial and must be labelled as such. No version newer than 2.0 was verified.

## NatureLM-audio: the model you can talk to

[NatureLM-audio](https://huggingface.co/EarthSpeciesProject/NatureLM-audio) is the Earth Species Project's audio-language foundation model, and it is the most ambitious of the three — and the one most often misunderstood. It links an audio encoder to a large language model built on LLaMA 3.1, so that a recording can be queried in plain English: "Which species are calling here?" It performs species classification, call-type and life-stage classification, detection, captioning, and counting, including zero-shot generalisation to species that were not in its training data. That last capability is the point of a foundation model: it is supposed to handle the unfamiliar, not only the familiar. But it is a **research model, not a translation device**. The Earth Species Project and independent commentators are explicit that it finds patterns in animal sound, not confirmed meanings, and no translation of animal communication has been claimed or achieved by it. Anyone who describes NatureLM-audio as a translator is misrepresenting it.

The paper is Robinson and colleagues, *NatureLM-audio: An Audio-Language Foundation Model for Bioacoustics*, [arXiv:2411.07186](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07186), November 2024. The training dataset is large by bioacoustic standards: **more than 26 million (audio, text) pairs, more than 2 million audio files, 22,100 hours of audio, and 44 bioacoustic tasks**, drawing on Xeno-canto, iNaturalist, Watkins, the Animal Sound Archive, and human speech, music, and environmental sound. On Hugging Face the training set, [EarthSpeciesProject/NatureLM-audio-training](https://huggingface.co/datasets/EarthSpeciesProject/NatureLM-audio-training), was verified at **26.4 million rows** by a scrape on 7 November 2026. The model is at [EarthSpeciesProject/NatureLM-audio](https://huggingface.co/EarthSpeciesProject/NatureLM-audio), and a no-code demo exists. **NatureLM-audio 2.0 was described as "to be released soon"** in a blog post of 30 September 2026, and model-merging work from a NeurIPS 2025 workshop, which improved F1 scores on unseen families by more than 200 percent relative to baseline, is folded into version 2.0. In July 2026 the project also launched **alp-data**, a shared data layer uniting more than 35 machine-learning-ready bioacoustic datasets.

NatureLM-audio's licence position is the least settled of the three models. The training data is composite: each sample carries its own licence in a `license` field, so the dataset has no single licence. The **model weights licence was not verified**; it was not stated on the dataset card scraped, and none should be asserted until the Hugging Face model card has been checked directly. Access is through Hugging Face and the public demo; no hosted API was found. **NatureLM is not connected to WAVE.** Its code lives in [earthspecies/NatureLM-audio](https://github.com/earthspecies/NatureLM-audio) (MIT, 108 stars, 1 November 2026), with a demo repository at [earthspecies/naturelm-audio-demo](https://github.com/earthspecies/naturelm-audio-demo). No public NatureLM repository exists outside the Earth Species Project. Two gaps remain: the version 2.0 release date, still pending, and any species-count claim — the project describes "thousands of species" without a verified number, and none is quoted here.

## The three models side by side

Put together, the comparison is straightforward. BirdNET is the proven classifier: the most deployed, the most directly usable, the one with the clearest licence split — MIT code, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 models, no hosted API — and the one WAVE actually runs. Its weakness is its fixed vocabulary: it knows the species it was trained on, and a sound outside that list is something it cannot name. Perch is the flexible foundation: an embedding model that makes new classifiers cheap to build, with the largest verified species coverage of the three at 14,597 species, Apache-2.0 code, and free distribution through Kaggle — but with almost no marine training data, a code repository that warns it is partly out of date, and no hosted API either. NatureLM-audio is the most general: a language model that can answer questions in words and generalise to unseen species, trained on the largest dataset of the three at 26.4 million pairs — but a research model with an unverified weights licence, no species-count claim, no hosted API, and a version 2.0 still awaited. None of the three offers a hosted API: every one has to be downloaded, run, and maintained by the user, and that cost — compute, storage, updates, licence compliance — is part of the real price of using them. And none of the three translates animal communication. BirdNET identifies, Perch embeds, NatureLM-audio describes. Translation, in the sense of recovering meaning, is not a capability any of them claims, and Project CETI is explicit that it has not achieved it.

## The archives: where the training data actually comes from

Models are only as good as their training data, and in bioacoustics that data is overwhelmingly public, community-built, and older than the models themselves. The archives below are the commons on which BirdNET, Perch, and NatureLM-audio all stand — public resources WAVE links to, not assets WAVE owns, each with its own licence, access route, and gaps.

### Xeno-canto: the community archive that trained everything

[Xeno-canto](https://xeno-canto.org/) is the community archive of wildlife sounds — birds, grasshoppers, bats, frogs, and land mammals — run by the Xeno-canto Foundation (Willem-Pier Vellinga, Bob Planqué, and Sander Pieterse). It is the principal training source for BirdNET, Merlin, Perch, and NatureLM-audio. Its current count, verified by a scrape of the homepage on 7 November 2026, is **1,111,111 recordings** — a milestone announced on the site's news feed, corroborated by the maintainers' page ("about 1,100,000 recordings of more than 13,400 species … shared by more than 13,000 recordists," 2026). It **supersedes the figure of roughly 1,040,000** correct in early November 2026; the archive grows by roughly 5,000 recordings a month, so a count without a date is already stale. It has been used in more than 6,000 publications, according to Google Scholar in 2026.

Recordings carry **individual Creative Commons licences**, most commonly CC BY-NC-SA, shown per recording. There is a free, open **API** at [xeno-canto.org/explore/api](https://xeno-canto.org/explore/api), with a Python wrapper, `xenopy`. New in 2026 are annotations using the "Annota-JSON" schema, exportable through the API, and a Spectrolipi integration; the site also notes bot pressure in late 2026. The exact species count on any given day was not verified — 13,400 and above is the maintainers' rounded figure. The central fact stands: if you want to know why BirdNET knows a particular bird, the likeliest answer is that a volunteer recordist uploaded it to Xeno-canto.

### The Macaulay Library: the oldest and largest scientific archive

The [Macaulay Library](https://www.macaulaylibrary.org/), part of the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, is the world's largest and oldest scientific archive of wildlife media. Founded in 1929 — the first recordings were made on 18 May 1929 in Ithaca — it is integrated with eBird, and, with Xeno-canto, is the other principal training source for BirdNET. Its current count, as of November 2026, is **3.2 million or more audio recordings, 84 million or more photographs, and 300,000 or more videos, covering 96 percent of the world's bird species**, according to the library's Wikipedia entry citing Cornell Lab sources and scraped on 7 November 2026. Cornell's own figures are consistent: a job posting describes "more than 77 million images, 3 million sound recordings, and 350,000 videos, from more than 80,000 contributors," and Cornell Alumni reported in March 2025 that more than 500,000 audio recordings were uploaded in 2024 alone. The library's 500,000th video was reached on 12 July 2026.

Two cautions attach to that figure. A secondary report from October 2026 said the library held **3.8 million sound recordings** in September 2026. That conflicts with 3.2 million and is **unverified** — it may reflect a different counting basis, such as uncatalogued mobile uploads — so this article keeps 3.2 million and above and flags the 3.8 million claim as unresolved, not as fact. The library is free to browse and listen to, and contributors retain copyright; the **exact current reuse and download terms were not verified**, so no licence is asserted here. The collection is also published through GBIF, at [gbif.org/es/dataset/7f6dd0f7-9ed4-49c0-bb71-b2a9c7fed9f1](https://www.gbif.org/es/dataset/7f6dd0f7-9ed4-49c0-bb71-b2a9c7fed9f1).

### Watkins: the historic marine archive, and a site that went dark

The William A. Watkins Marine Mammal Sound Database, at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, is the historic reference collection of marine mammal vocalisations, built over more than forty years by William Watkins with William Schevill. It spans seven decades, from the 1940s to the 2000s, including the first recordings of 51 marine mammal species. Its holdings, as described by Sayigh and colleagues in 2016 (*Proceedings of Meetings on Acoustics*, volume 27, article 040013), are **roughly 2,000 unique recordings of more than 60 marine mammal species, with more than 15,000 annotated digital sound clips**. A curated "Best of" subset holds **1,694 high-quality cuts across 32 species**; 15,000 and above is the full archive, 1,694 the curated subset.

Watkins also carries the most important access caveat in this article. The official site — `cis.whoi.edu/science/B/whalesounds/`, with a mirror at `whoicf2.whoi.edu` — was **offline as of 19 September 2026**, both hosts redirecting to a maintenance page. Working fallbacks are the **BEANS Google Cloud Storage zip** (`storage.googleapis.com/ml-bioacoustics-datasets/watkins.zip`, 882 megabytes, WAV files plus annotations, 31 classes) and Hugging Face mirrors, including `DBD-research-group/beans_watkins` and `ivangtorre/watkins-marine-mammal-full-cuts` (15,200 recordings, 54 species per its card). The Kaggle mirror of the "Best of" database **returns a 404 and must not be linked**. Watkins material is free to download with no registration under the site terms when live; no per-clip Creative Commons licence was verified. Whether the WHOI site is back online after September 2026 was not re-checked. For WAVE, Watkins matters because marine classification is cross-reference against Watkins only — not direct classification.

### iNaturalist Sounds: the citizen-science flood, with a licence to read carefully

[iNaturalist](https://www.inaturalist.org/), the global citizen-science platform run jointly by the California Academy of Sciences and the National Geographic Society, attaches audio recordings as evidence to observations. Sounds are one of three separately licensed content types per observation — data, photographs, and audio — and are queryable through the API (`sounds=true`, `sound_license=` filters). The solid sourced figure is that **571,698 iNaturalist recordings** were in Perch 2.0's training data, drawn from research-grade observations via GBIF and downloaded in March 2025. A **current platform-wide total of sounds was not verified**: iNaturalist publishes observation totals, not a sounds total, and no total is quoted here.

The licence position deserves care. The **default for new accounts is CC BY-NC for all three content types**, and users may choose CC0, CC BY, CC BY-NC-SA, CC BY-NC-ND, CC BY-ND, CC BY-SA, or all rights reserved — licences can differ within a single observation. Only CC-licensed media are shared with GBIF. Open-licensed media are bulk-accessible through the **iNaturalist Open Data programme on AWS** (`inaturalist-open-data`). As of July 2026, roughly 23 percent of observations were not under an open observation licence and could not be redistributed through GBIF (Callaghan and colleagues, *BioScience*, 2026, doi:10.1093/biosci/biag135). The commercial-use implications of the CC BY-NC default for WAVE are a decision to be made, not a fact to be assumed.

### The Animal Sound Archive, Berlin: old, large, and gated in practice

The Animal Sound Archive — the Tierstimmenarchiv — at the Museum für Naturkunde Berlin was founded in **1951** by Günter Tembrock. It is one of the world's oldest and third-largest bioacoustic archives, holding recordings of birds, mammals, fish, amphibians, reptiles, and insects. Its holdings are **roughly 120,000 to 130,000 recordings**: the GBIF dataset description says "around 130,000 records of animal voices," and the museum's 75th-anniversary report of October 2026 says "**more than 120,000 recordings … from approximately 1,800 bird species and 580 mammal species**." The archive celebrated its 75th anniversary with a colloquium on 7 November 2026. Perch 2.0 used 33,859 Tierstimmenarchiv recordings in April 2025.

Access is where the archive is hardest to pin down. Its search portal is at `suche.tierstimmenarchiv.de`, as cited in the Perch 2.0 paper, and the dataset is published through GBIF (doi:10.15468/0bpalr). Older sources describe representative sections of more than 10,000 recordings as listenable online, with complete recordings available on request for scientific purposes — but a 2025 review table lists access as "**Contact required**," which conflicts with the "open access" description, so bulk access should be treated as gated or unclear. The museum's research page URL **returned a 404 when scraped on 7 November 2026**; use the search portal and the GBIF record instead. **No licence statement was found**, and none is asserted here.

### GBIF: the aggregator, not an archive

The Global Biodiversity Information Facility — [GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/) — is the intergovernmental open-data infrastructure, founded in 2001, that aggregates species occurrence records from museums, citizen-science platforms, and agencies using the Darwin Core standard. It is an **index and aggregator, not a sound archive itself** — but it is where the open-licensed media of iNaturalist, Xeno-canto, the Macaulay Library, and the Animal Sound Archive become jointly searchable and downloadable. Its scale is **3.8 billion or more occurrence records** as of August 2026 (Miller and colleagues, "The Global Biodiversity Information Facility at 25," *Biodiversity Data Journal*, volume 14, article e208528, October 2026): 70 participating countries, data providers in more than 120 countries, 2,700 institutions. Fewer than 5 percent of records are machine observations. The **current number of audio-enabled records was not verified**: the last sourced figure is from 2017, when Xeno-canto's addition of 170,041 audio records more than doubled a base of roughly 100,000 — nine years stale, and not quoted as current. Access is free and open, with data under CC0, CC BY, or CC BY-NC as set by each publisher; analytics are at [gbif.org/analytics/global](https://www.gbif.org/analytics/global).

### The Earth Species Library, Project CETI, and the rest of the commons

The Earth Species Project's **Earth Species Library**, at [github.com/earthspecies/library](https://github.com/earthspecies/library), is an open repository of machine-learning-ready, primarily acoustic datasets on GitHub, curated and documented for training bioacoustic models. It is distinct from the NatureLM-audio training set, though related. Examples include a Bird Song dataset of 686 recordings with 87 labels, and an Egyptian Fruit Bat dataset of 300,000 unique vocalisations with 90,000 labelled annotations. The project notes it holds "many more data sets that we have not yet been able to make public." One caution: the project's website Library page **returned "Page not found" when scraped on 7 November 2026**, so the GitHub repository is the live home, not the old website URL. No aggregate total for the Library's dataset count or hours was published, and no per-dataset licences beyond the general public-domain and Creative Commons statement were verified.

[Project CETI](https://www.projectceti.org/) — the Cetacean Translation Initiative — is a nonprofit founded in 2020, based in Dominica, with eight institutions and roughly fifty scientists, applying machine learning, robotics, linguistics, and underwater acoustics to sperm whale communication. It studies codas, the rhythmic click patterns sperm whales produce. **No translation has been achieved**, and CETI's researchers are explicit about that. In May 2024 the project published its "sperm whale phonetic alphabet": **8,719 codas from roughly 60 individual whales** analysed with machine learning, yielding **156 distinct codas** with combinatorial structure (*Nature Communications*, volume 15, article 3393, doi:10.1038/s41467-024-47221-8). Later work found vowel- and diphthong-like spectral patterns in codas (November 2025), published "The phonology of sperm whale coda vowels" (April 2026, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, volume 293, article 20252994), and recorded the first complete drone-plus-hydrophone record of a sperm whale birth (March 2026). In December 2025 an open-source bio-logger from Harvard SEAS was published (Vogt and colleagues, *PLOS ONE*, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0337093). CETI is a member of the **AWS Open Data Program** and partners with Google Research and Microsoft Research. What is public is the papers, the bio-logger designs and code, and selected footage and audio released with publications. **No verified public bulk download of the coda corpus was found**: the AWS Open Data dataset was not located or confirmed. Do not claim the whale recordings are downloadable. The size of CETI's total recorded corpus, beyond the 8,719-coda study set, was also not verified.

## What WAVE actually uses today — and what would have to change

Today, WAVE's live predictor is BirdNET, and Perch and NatureLM-audio are not connected. That is not a judgement on them; it is a statement of what is running. Connecting either would mean downloading and self-hosting the model, resolving its licence — CC BY-NC-SA for BirdNET models, Apache-2.0 code for Perch with an unverified Kaggle weights licence, an unverified weights licence for NatureLM-audio — and, for Perch, addressing the near-absence of marine training data before trusting it with ocean sound. The benchmarks that would guide such a decision exist: **BEANS**, the Benchmark of Animal Sounds ([github.com/earthspecies/beans](https://github.com/earthspecies/beans)), covering twelve datasets across birds, mammals, amphibians, and insects, with BEANS-Zero as the zero-shot benchmark for NatureLM-audio; and **BirdSet**, built on roughly 520,000 Xeno-canto recordings for training and 400-plus hours of annotated passive recordings for testing ([huggingface.co/datasets/DBD-research-group/BirdSet](https://huggingface.co/datasets/DBD-research-group/BirdSet)). Google's reef adaptation, **SurfPerch** ([kaggle.com/models/google/surfperch](https://www.kaggle.com/models/google/surfperch)), and its whale classifiers on Kaggle, plus **DolphinGemma** — Google DeepMind's roughly 400-million-parameter audio-language model trained on forty years of Atlantic spotted dolphin recordings, announced in April 2025 — are also worth naming with their status attached: DeepMind's own page, accessed in November 2026, says DolphinGemma is "currently in development" and will be openly available on release, and Wikipedia likewise notes that no model or data have been publicly released. **Do not claim DolphinGemma is released or available.**

## Glossary

- **Audio-language model:** a model that links an audio encoder to a language model, so recordings can be queried in plain English.
- **BEANS:** Benchmark of Animal Sounds — a suite of twelve bioacoustic datasets for comparing models.
- **Coda:** rhythmic click pattern of sperm whales; CETI's unit of study.
- **Embedding:** a compact numerical fingerprint of a sound, from which small classifiers can be trained.
- **Foundation model:** a large model trained on broad data, adapted to many tasks.
- **Hydrophone:** underwater microphone.
- **Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM):** recording sound without emitting it, typically with autonomous recorders left in place.
- **Zero-shot:** a task for a species or class not seen in training.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which model does WAVE actually use?**
BirdNET. Perch and NatureLM-audio are not connected to WAVE.

**Is BirdNET's species count 6,522 or 11,000+?**
Both — for different generations. Version 2.4 (production, March 2025) covers 6,522 bird species; the V3.0 generation in preview and live apps covers 11,000+ species.

**Can any of these models translate animal communication?**
No. BirdNET identifies, Perch embeds, NatureLM-audio describes. Project CETI is explicit that no whale translation has been achieved.

**Is DolphinGemma available?**
Not as verified in November 2026. DeepMind's page said it was "currently in development."

**Are the archives owned by WAVE?**
No. They are public resources WAVE links to, each with its own licence and access terms.
